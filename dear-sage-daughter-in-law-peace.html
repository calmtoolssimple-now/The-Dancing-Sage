How Can I Get Along Better with My Daughter-in-Law?

You don't see eye to eye, and the visits feel tense. How to build a warmer relationship without biting your tongue forever.

← All questions and answers

A question the Sage hears often

My daughter-in-law and I just don't click. She does things very differently from how I raised my son, and I can feel the tension whenever we visit. I don't want to lose my place in their family, or my grandchildren. What can I do?

In short: Treat her home as her territory, offer help rather than advice, and build a relationship with her directly, not only through your son. Small, steady kindnesses will do more than one big conversation.

The Sage replies…

Remember whose kitchen it is

Here is a hard truth wrapped in a soft shawl: in their home, she sets the rules. Even when you have forty years of experience and she is doing it the long way round. Especially then.

Most daughters-in-law aren't rejecting you. They are trying to build their own family and feel judged while doing it. The fastest way to soften her is to make her feel that you're on her team.

Offer help, not advice

Advice, even good advice, can sound like criticism to someone who is tired and trying hard. Help sounds like love. "Would it help if I took the children to the park for an hour?" will win you more than "You know, when Daniel was little, I used to..."

If she asks your opinion, give it gladly. If she doesn't, keep it in your pocket. You'll be surprised how often she asks once she feels safe.

Build your own friendship with her

If every conversation goes through your son, she stays "his mother" rather than becoming someone she knows. Send her a note when you see something she'd like. Ask about her work, her hobbies, her own family. Compliment her mothering in front of others; it travels.

And if something truly needs saying, say it to her calmly, privately and once. Never through your son, and never in front of the children.

With a wink and a twirl,
— The Dancing Sage

Try this

One small kindness this week

Send her a short message that has nothing to do with your son or the grandchildren. A photo of something that made you think of her, or a genuine compliment about something she did well.

A question to take with you: What would make her feel I'm on her side, rather than watching her?
