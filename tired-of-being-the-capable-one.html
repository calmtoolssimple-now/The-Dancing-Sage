When You're Tired of Being the Capable One

Some mornings, holding everything together feels less like love and more like a habit you can't put down.

← Back to the Wisdom Library

In short: If you're the one who does everything, it may be partly because nobody else is asked to, and partly because it's hard to let things be done differently. You don't have to drop it all. Hand over one small job, let it be done imperfectly, and see what happens.

You know that feeling you get on a Tuesday morning?

You're putting lunches together, making sure everyone has matching socks, searching for the thing somebody lost and trying, unsuccessfully, to drink a cup of coffee before running out the door.

And somewhere in the middle of all that rushing, you suddenly think: Why am I doing this?

Why didn't I let the fifteen-year-old make his own lunch, or go without one if he couldn't be bothered to make it himself?

Why didn't I sit down for a few minutes and enjoy breakfast with my youngest, instead of spending the whole morning rushing everyone along?

Why didn't we make room for one small family moment?

I really dislike Tuesday mornings sometimes.

Am I needed, or am I in charge?

Then another thought creeps in. If I don't do all these things, they won't get done, at least not the way I like them done.

And there it is.

Is it really that everyone needs me to hold the household together? Or is part of this about control?

I think, for many of us, it's a bit of both. Being capable is a real strength. It's how families get fed, how forms get signed and how everyone arrives more or less on time. But a strength can quietly turn into a trap when nobody else ever gets the chance to try.

The more we do, the less others learn to do. And the less they do, the more it seems we have to.

What happens if you stop?

If I stop helping the children and my better half with every little thing, will the house fall apart, along with everyone's sanity?

Or will they simply learn to find their own socks?

In my experience, it goes something like this: a few bumpy days, one forgotten lunch, a bit of grumbling and then, surprisingly often, people manage. A fifteen-year-old can make a sandwich. A grown man can find the school form. And children who are trusted with small jobs grow into adults who can look after themselves. Letting go isn't failing them. It's part of raising them.

Five jobs you could hand over this week

  1. Lunches. Anyone old enough to use a knife can make their own, the night before. Keep the bread, fillings and containers together so it's easy.
  2. The sock hunt. One basket per person for clean laundry. What happens after that is their business.
  3. One whole job for your partner. Not "help with", but own it completely: school forms, the bins, or Tuesday's dinner.
  4. A launch pad by the door. A hook or basket for each person's bag, keys and shoes, so you're not the household's lost-and-found.
  5. Your own breakfast. Ten minutes, sitting down, with a hot cup of coffee. Put it on the list as if it were a job, because it is.

How to let go without it turning into a battle

  • Say it once, calmly, ahead of time. "From Monday, lunches are yours." Not in the middle of a rushed morning.
  • Hand over the whole job, not half of it. Half a job still lives in your head.
  • Don't redo it. If the towels are folded wrong, they are still folded. Redoing it tells everyone it wasn't worth trying.
  • Expect it to get worse before it gets better. That's normal. Hold steady for two weeks.
  • Notice and say thank you. People keep doing what gets noticed.

Make room for one small moment

What I really wanted on that Tuesday morning wasn't a perfectly run household. It was a few minutes at the table with my youngest. That's the point of handing things over: not to do less for the sake of it, but to make room for the moments we keep missing.

Try this tomorrow: Pick one Tuesday-morning job and hand it to someone tonight. Then use the minutes it frees up to sit down with your coffee, and with whoever is at the table.

I honestly don't know whether the house would fall apart if I stopped doing everything. I suspect it wouldn't.

But I can tell you this: sometimes, I am tired of being the capable one.

Just sometimes.

And I've decided that's allowed.

— The Dancing Sage
