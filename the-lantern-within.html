The Lantern Within

Even on dim days, there is still a small lantern within us waiting to be noticed.

← Back to the Wisdom Library

In short: Grief, burnout and long uncertainty can make life feel without light. Yet a small part of you keeps choosing to try, even when trying feels pointless. That quiet hope isn't denial. It is what carries you through, and it only needs a little shelter and time.

There are seasons that feel entirely without light. Grief does this. Burnout does this. Long stretches of uncertainty do this, dimming everything until the days start to blur into each other, indistinguishable and grey.

But even in those seasons, something in us tends to keep burning, small and stubborn, refusing to fully go out.

A light you only notice in the dark

It is easy to miss it. A lantern in daylight looks like nothing at all — just glass and a small flame you'd never notice unless you were specifically looking. It is only in real darkness that its light becomes obvious, becomes necessary, becomes the thing that lets you take the next step without stumbling.

That small, stubborn light in you is not naivety. It is not denial of how hard things actually are. It is simply the part of you that keeps choosing to try, quietly, underneath everything else, even when trying feels pointless.

Sheltering your lantern

You may not be able to see it clearly right now. That does not mean it isn't there. Lanterns don't need to be seen to keep burning. They only need a little fuel, and a little shelter from the wind, and time.

If today has felt dim, this is not a sign that your light has gone out. It is only a sign that you are in a season dark enough to finally notice it was there all along.

Shelter your lantern today. It has been carrying you further than you know.

— The Dancing Sage

Try this in everyday life

Make a small list of things worth returning to

A personal list of familiar pleasures can be useful on days when inventing a new activity feels like work.

Write five specific things you have enjoyed before: the first chapter of a favourite book, a particular song, colouring leaves, phoning one friend, or sitting beside the kitchen window. Make each item small enough to begin without a shopping trip. Keep the list inside a book or beside your pencils.

Choose one and try it briefly. If it is not welcome today, put it aside without turning the result into a judgement about yourself. Preferences change. Update the list with what actually suits you, including invitations to spend time with other people. These ideas are gentle options, not a requirement to feel cheerful or manage every difficult day alone.

A question to take with you: What familiar pleasure asks very little of me to begin?
