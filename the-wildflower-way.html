The Wildflower Way

A wildflower does not apologize for blooming in an unexpected place — a reflection on self-compassion.

← Back to the Wisdom Library

In short: Self-compassion isn't self-indulgence, and it isn't something you have to earn. Like a wildflower that blooms wherever it takes root, you are allowed to be exactly where you are: unfinished, a little unruly and still growing.

Nobody plants a wildflower.

It arrives on its own terms, in the crack of a sidewalk, the ditch beside the highway, the neglected corner of a field no one thought to tend. And it blooms there anyway, fully, without checking first whether the location was appropriate.

Why we keep apologizing

We rarely extend ourselves that same permission.

Instead we apologize for the shape our lives have taken. For healing slower than we hoped. For needing help. For the parts of us that grew a little wild because no one was there to prune them into something tidier.

The wildflower does not compare its petals to the roses in someone's garden. It has no interest in a garden at all. It is too busy being exactly what it is, in the one place it happened to take root.

Self-compassion is not indulgence

Self-compassion often gets mistaken for self-indulgence, as though being gentle with yourself is a luxury you have to earn first. But a wildflower doesn't earn its right to bloom. It simply blooms, because that is what living things do when nothing is choking them for it.

You do not need permission to be exactly where you are right now, unfinished, a little unruly, still growing in directions no one planned for you.

Let today be the day you stop apologizing for the shape you took to survive.

You were never meant to look like the flowers in someone else's garden. You were only ever meant to bloom where you actually are.

— The Dancing Sage

Try this in everyday life

Give your own taste a place at the table

Self-kindness can begin with an everyday preference that does not need defending.

Choose something small by your own taste: a colour for a scarf, the cup you drink from, a book to borrow, or the music you play while tidying. Notice whether you begin explaining why it is sensible, fashionable or useful. For this choice, let liking it be enough.

If you are colouring, choose three pencils you enjoy together rather than searching for the correct palette. Try them in a corner or on spare paper. You are allowed to dislike the result and choose again. The point is to give your preferences room to exist, not to force yourself to admire everything you make.

A question to take with you: What would I choose if I did not have to make it look impressive to anyone else?
