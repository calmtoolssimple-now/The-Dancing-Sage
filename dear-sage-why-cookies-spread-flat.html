A question the Sage hears often

My chocolate chip cookies keep spreading into thin, flat, greasy puddles, even though I'm using the same recipe my mother did. What am I doing wrong?

In short: The usual culprits are butter that's too soft or melted, too little flour, warm baking sheets, or old leavening. Chill the dough, measure flour carefully, and use cool pans.

The Sage replies…

Don't worry; the recipe isn't broken

Flat cookies are one of the most common kitchen frustrations, and the good news is that they're nearly always fixable. Usually it's one small thing that has crept in, not your baking skills.

The usual culprits

Butter that's too warm. "Softened" butter should still feel cool and hold a fingerprint, not look shiny or greasy. If it's melted, or your kitchen is warm, the cookies spread before they set.

Not quite enough flour. How you fill the measuring cup changes how much flour you get. Scooping straight from the bag packs more in; spooning it in lightly and levelling gives less. If your mother scooped and you spoon, your dough may be a little short of flour. A tablespoon or two more can help. Weighing flour on a kitchen scale is the most reliable fix of all.

Warm baking sheets. Putting dough on a hot pan from the last batch starts the butter melting at once. Let pans cool, or rotate between two.

Greased pans. Extra grease encourages spreading. Use parchment paper instead.

Old baking soda or baking powder. If the box has been open longer than you can remember, replace it.

The Sage's favourite fix: chill the dough

Pop the scooped dough balls in the fridge for 30 to 60 minutes before baking. The butter firms up, the flour soaks up moisture, and the cookies hold their shape and taste richer too. It's the closest thing to magic in a kitchen.

And if a batch still comes out thin? Call them "lace cookies," serve them with tea, and nobody will know.

Try this

Test one change at a time

Next time you bake, change only one thing: chill the dough first. If they still spread, next time also add a tablespoon of flour. Changing one thing at a time tells you exactly what your kitchen needs.
